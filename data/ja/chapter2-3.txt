To tell the truth, I did not really want to eat the ozoni all that much. In fact, the more closely I stared at the bottom of the bowl, the more creeped out I felt, and I lost my appetite. At that moment, if O-san had opened the back door, or if I had heard the children's footsteps approaching from the inner room, I would have abandoned the bowl without regret, and the thought of ozoni would not have crossed my mind again until next year. But no one came. No matter how much I hesitated, no one came. I felt as if I were being hurried along: "Aren't you going to eat it? Eat it already!" Looking into the bowl, I prayed for someone to come quickly. Still, no one came. I finally had to eat the ozoni. At last, dropping the weight of my entire body toward the bottom of the bowl, I snapped my teeth into a corner of the mochi. Since I had bitten down with such force, any ordinary thing should have been bitten through—but I was shocked! When I tried to pull back my teeth, thinking I'd finished, I couldn't. When I tried to bite again, I found myself paralyzed. By the time I realized that mochi is a demon, it was already too late. Like someone who has fallen into a marsh and sinks deeper the more they struggle to pull their leg out, the more I chewed, the heavier my mouth became, and my teeth would not move. There was a sensation of chewing, but only that; I simply couldn't finish it off. Meitei, the aesthetician, once remarked that my master is an "indivisible" man, and he certainly had a point. This mochi, just like my master, was utterly indivisible. No matter how much I chewed, it felt as if there would be no end to it, like trying to divide ten by three for all eternity. In the midst of this agony, I unwittingly arrived at a second truth: "All animals instinctively foresee the suitability of things." I had already discovered two truths, but since the mochi was stuck to me, I felt no pleasure at all. My teeth felt as if they were being absorbed into the flesh of the mochi, and it hurt enough to make them feel like they were being pulled out. I had to bite through and escape quickly, or O-san would come. The children's singing seemed to have stopped; they would surely come running into the kitchen. In my extreme distress, I tried wagging my tail around, but it was of no use. I tried pricking up my ears and laying them flat, but that failed too. Come to think of it, ears and tails have absolutely no connection to mochi. Realizing that wagging, pricking, and flattening were all a waste of effort, I stopped. At last, the idea occurred to me that I must use my front paws to brush the mochi off. First, I raised the right one and tried to stroke around my mouth. A mere stroke was not going to divide it. This time, I extended the left one and tried to draw a rapid circle around my mouth. No such spell would break the demon's hold. Thinking that patience was key, I moved them alternately, but my teeth remained suspended in the mochi just as before. "Oh, to hell with it!" I thought, using both paws at once. Then, miraculously, for this moment alone, I was able to stand on my two hind legs. I felt like I was no longer a cat. Whether I was a cat or not, once things had come to this, I didn't care; with the spirit of "keep going until the mochi-demon falls off," I scratched frantically all over my face. The movement of my front paws was so violent that I frequently lost my balance and began to topple over. Since I had to keep my balance with my hind legs every time I tilted, I couldn't stay in one spot and went leaping all around the kitchen. I myself wondered how I was able to stand so skillfully. A third truth suddenly appeared before me: "In the face of danger, one can achieve what is normally impossible. This is called divine grace." Having fortunately received this divine grace, I was fighting the mochi-demon with all my might when I felt as if footsteps were approaching and someone was coming from the inner room. Thinking it would be a disaster to be seen now, I scurried around the kitchen more frantically than ever. The footsteps grew closer. Ah, what a pity; the divine grace was not quite enough. I was finally spotted by the children. "Look! The cat's eating the ozoni and dancing!" one cried out in a loud voice. The first to hear this was O-san. She threw aside her battledore and shuttlecock and came flying in from the back, shouting, "Oh my goodness!" The mistress, in her crested crepe kimono, said, "What a disgusting cat." Even the master came out of his study and said, "You idiot!" Only the children thought it was amusing. And then, as if by agreement, everyone burst into fits of laughter. I was angry, I was in pain, but I couldn't stop my dance. I was in a fix. Just as the laughter seemed about to subside, the five-year-old girl said, "Mother, isn't the cat quite something?"—and as if to catch the receding tide, they all roared with laughter again. I have seen and heard much of human lack of sympathy, but I never felt it as bitterly as at that moment. Finally, the divine grace vanished somewhere, and I was reduced to the pathetic state of being on all fours, rolling my eyes in distress. Apparently even they felt it was a pity to leave me to my fate, for the master ordered O-san, "Well, take the mochi off for him." O-san looked at the mistress with an expression that seemed to say, "Can't we let him dance a bit more?" The mistress wanted to see more of the dance, but had no desire to see me killed, so she remained silent. "Take it off or he'll die. Take it off quickly," the master turned to the maid again. With a listless face, like someone awakened from a dream while halfway through a feast, O-san grabbed the mochi and gave it a hard yank. Not to be like Kangetsu-kun, but I thought all my front teeth would break. Whether it was painful or not—she was mercilessly pulling at teeth that were firmly embedded in the mochi; it was unbearable. By the time I had experienced the fourth truth—"All pleasure must pass through hardship"—and looked around in a daze, the family had already gone back into the inner room.
