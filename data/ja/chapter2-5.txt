When I returned home, the sitting room felt unusually spring-like, and even the master's laughter sounded cheerful. Wondering what was happening, I climbed up from the open veranda and approached my master to find a stranger visiting. He was a young man who looked like a very serious student, with his hair neatly parted, wearing a crested cotton haori and Ogura hakama. On the corner of the master's brazier, beside a Shunkei-lacquered cigarette case, lay a calling card that read "Ochi Kochi, introduced by Mizushima Kangetsu," so I learned both the guest’s name and the fact that he was a friend of Kangetsu-kun. Their conversation was already in progress, so I couldn't quite follow the beginning, but it seemed to concern the aesthetician Meitei-kun, whom I introduced previously.

"He told me there was an interesting plan afoot and insisted I come along," the guest said composedly.

"An 'interesting plan' regarding going to a Western restaurant for lunch?" the master asked, refilling his tea and pushing the cup toward the guest.

"Well, I didn't understand what the plan was at the time, but since it was him, I assumed he had some amusing trick up his sleeve..."

"And did you go with him? I see."

"The thing is, I was quite surprised."

As if to say "I told you so," the master gave my head a thud as I sat on his lap. It hurt a little.

"It was likely some foolish charade again. That's a habit of his," the master said, suddenly recalling the Andrea del Sarto incident.

"Hehe. He said to me, 'Let’s eat something unusual, shall we?'"

"And what did you eat?"

"First, while looking at the menu, he gave a long discourse on various cuisines."

"Before ordering?"

"Yes."

"And then?"

"Then he tilted his head, looked at the waiter, and said, 'It seems there’s nothing out of the ordinary here.' When the waiter, not wanting to be outdone, suggested roast duck or veal chops, Meitei-san replied, 'I didn't come all this way just to eat such "commonplace" fare.' Since the waiter didn't understand what he meant by 'commonplace,' he just looked puzzled and remained silent."

"I imagine he did."

"Then he turned to me and said, with an air of great importance, 'If you go to France or England, you can find plenty of dishes in the "Tenmei" or "Manyo" styles, but in Japan, everything is like it was pressed from a single mold; I never feel like stepping into a Western restaurant.' Tell me, has he actually been abroad?"

"Meitei go abroad? Not a chance. He has the money and the time, so he could go whenever he liked, I suppose. He was probably just treating his future plans as if they were past experiences for the sake of a joke," the master said, laughing at what he considered his own clever remark. The guest did not seem particularly impressed.

"Is that so? I wondered when he had found the time to go abroad, so I was listening quite earnestly. Especially since he described things like 'slug soup' and 'frog stew' as if he had seen them with his own eyes."

"He likely heard it from someone. He’s a master at telling lies, after all."

"So it seems," the guest said, gazing at the narcissus in the vase. He looked a trifle disappointed.

"So, was that the 'plan'?" the master pressed.

"No, that was merely the introduction. The main part was yet to come."

"Hmph," the master uttered an inquisitive exclamation.

"Then he said to me, 'Since we can't get slug or frog even if we wanted to, why don't we settle for something like "Tochimenbo," my friend?' Without thinking, I simply agreed that it sounded like a good idea."

"Heh, 'Tochimenbo' is a strange one."

"Yes, quite strange, but he was so serious that it simply didn't occur to me," the guest said, appearing to apologize to the master for his own lack of caution.

"And then what happened?" the master asked indifferently, showing no sympathy for the guest's apology.

"Then he said to the waiter, 'Hey, bring us two servings of Tochimenbo.' The waiter asked, 'Did you mean "mince-ball," sir?' but Meitei-san, with an even more serious face, corrected him: 'Not mince-ball, Tochimenbo.'"

"I see. Does such a dish as Tochimenbo actually exist?"

"Well, I thought it was a bit odd myself, but he was so calm, and he's such an expert on Western things—and at the time I fully believed he had been abroad—so I chimed in and told the waiter, 'Yes, Tochimenbo, Tochimenbo!'"

"And what did the waiter do?"

"Thinking back on it now, it was truly comical. He pondered for a while and then said, 'I'm very sorry, sir, but we are fresh out of Tochimenbo today. We can, however, prepare two servings of mince-balls immediately.' Meitei-san looked terribly disappointed and said, 'Then it was hardly worth coming all this way. Is there no way you could manage to fix us some Tochimenbo?' He handed the waiter a twenty-sen silver coin, and the waiter said, 'Well, in that case, I shall go and consult with the chef,' and disappeared into the back."

"He certainly seemed to have a strong craving for Tochimenbo."

"After a while, the waiter returned and said, 'We are truly sorry, sir. If you order it, we can make it, but it will take some time.' Meitei-san remained perfectly composed and said, 'Well, it's the New Year and we have plenty of time, so let’s wait and eat it,' and he took a cigar from his pocket and began puffing away. Having no choice, I took a copy of the *Nippon* newspaper from my pocket and began to read. Then the waiter went back to consult again."

"It seems to have been quite a troublesome process," the master said, leaning forward as if reading a war dispatch.

"Then the waiter came out again and said, with an air of regret, 'The ingredients for Tochimenbo are currently quite scarce, and we can't find them even at Kameya or at Number 15 in Yokohama, so I'm afraid we're out of luck for the time being.' Meitei-san turned to me and repeated over and over, 'That is a problem, isn't it? After we came all this way.' Since I couldn't just remain silent, I played along and said, 'It is indeed regrettable, most regrettable.'"

"Quite so," the master agreed. What was "quite so," I did not know.

"The waiter also seemed to feel bad and said, 'Please do come again when the ingredients arrive.' When Meitei-san asked what ingredients were used, the waiter just went 'hehehe' and didn't answer. When Meitei-san pressed him, asking, 'The material is surely haiku poets of the Japanese school, isn't it?' the waiter replied, 'Yes, quite so, sir, and that is why we can't buy any even in Yokohama these days, much to our regret!'"

"Hahaha! So that was the punchline? That’s marvelous!" the master laughed more loudly than usual. His knees shook, and I nearly fell off. The master laughed on, unconcerned. It seemed he was suddenly delighted to learn that he wasn't the only one who had fallen victim to an "Andrea del Sarto."

"Then, when the two of us stepped outside, he was quite triumphant, saying, 'Well, how about that? Didn't it go well? Using "Tochimenbo" as the subject was quite a hit, wasn't it?' I said I was deeply impressed and took my leave, but in truth, lunch had been so delayed that I was famished and quite weakened."

"That must have been a nuisance for you," the master said, finally showing some sympathy. I had no objection to that sentiment either. The conversation lapsed for a moment, and the sound of my purring reached the ears of both host and guest.
