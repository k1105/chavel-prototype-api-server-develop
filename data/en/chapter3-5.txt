The master said with a dissatisfied air, First of all, she has a face I can't stand, to which Meitei immediately agreed, adding, That nose takes up its post in the center of her face as if it’s someone important. And it's crooked too, the master said. A bit of a hunchback, isn't it? A hunchbacked nose is perhaps a bit too eccentric, Meitei laughed. It’s a face that would plague a husband, the master grumbled, still sounding bitter. It's a face that went unsold in the nineteenth century and now sits as shopworn stock in the twentieth, Meitei said in his peculiar way. Just then, the mistress emerged from the inner room and, with a woman’s intuition, warned them, If you speak so much ill of her, the rickshaw man's wife will surely tell on you again. A little telling on us might be good medicine, Ma'am. Still, slandering someone's appearance is rather low; it's not as if anyone chooses to have a nose like that—and since she is a woman, you are being far too harsh, she said, defending the woman's nose while indirectly defending her own features. Harsh? That’s no woman; she’s a fool. Isn't that right, Meitei-kun? A fool she may be, but she’s quite a formidable one; she gave you quite a scratching, didn't she? What on earth does she take a teacher for, anyway? She takes you for something on the level of the rickshaw man out back. To be respected by someone like that, you simply must become a Doctor. It’s your own fault for not having become one yet. Isn't that so, Ma'am? Meitei laughed, turning to the mistress. A Doctor? That’s quite out of the question for him, she said, having given up on her husband as well. Even I might become one eventually, so don't be so quick to look down on me. You wouldn't know, but in the old days a man named Isocrates wrote a great work at ninety-four. Sophocles shocked the world with a masterpiece at nearly a hundred. Simonides wrote exquisite poetry at eighty. Even I... Don't be ridiculous; how could someone with your stomach trouble live that long? the mistress said, having already calculated her husband's lifespan. How rude—go ask Dr. Amagi. It’s because you make me wear this wrinkled black cotton haori and these patched clothes that a woman like that mocks me. From tomorrow, I’m wearing something like what Meitei wears, so get it out. Get it out? We have no such splendid robes. The only reason Mrs. Kaneda became polite to Mr. Meitei was because she heard his uncle’s name. It wasn't the clothes' fault, the mistress said, skillfully shifting the blame.

Hearing the word uncle, the master suddenly asked as if remembering, This is the first I've heard of you having an uncle. You've never mentioned him before; does he actually exist? Meitei, as if waiting for the question, replied, Oh, that uncle—he's a terribly stubborn old fellow. He’s been living on continuously from the nineteenth century to this day, he said, looking at both the master and mistress. Ohoho! You say such funny things. Where does he live? He lives in Shizuoka, but he doesn't just live there. He lives with a topknot on his head, which is quite impressive. When told to wear a hat, he boasts, I’ve reached this age and never felt cold enough to need one. When told to sleep longer because it's cold, he says, Four hours of sleep is plenty for a human; sleeping more than that is a luxury, and he gets up while it's still dark. He brags that he’s trained for years to shorten his sleep to four hours and says he’s delighted to have finally reached a state where he can wake up at will. It’s only natural for a man of sixty-seven to be unable to sleep. It has nothing to do with training, yet he believes he’s succeeded through sheer willpower. And whenever he goes out, he always carries an iron fan. What for? I have no idea, he just carries it. Perhaps he thinks of it as a substitute for a cane. But something strange happened the other day, Meitei said, turning to the mistress. Oh? she replied noncommittally. This spring, he suddenly sent a letter telling me to send a bowler hat and a frock coat immediately. I was surprised and wrote back to ask, and he replied that he was going to wear them himself. He ordered me to procure them urgently so they would be ready for a victory celebration in Shizuoka on the twenty-third. But the funny thing was his instructions: Buy a hat of a reasonable size, and order the suit from Daimaru by estimating the measurements. Does Daimaru even make Western suits these days? No, the old man confused it with Shirokiya. But isn't it impossible to estimate measurements? That’s my uncle for you. So what did you do? Having no choice, I estimated and sent them. That was reckless of you. Did they fit? Well, somehow or other he managed. When I checked the local newspaper, it said that on the day, Mr. Makiyama appeared in an unusual frock coat, carrying his usual iron fan... So he didn't let go of the iron fan, it seems. Yes, I think I'll put the iron fan in his coffin when he dies. Still, it was good that the hat and suit fit reasonably well. That's where you're wrong. Just as I was feeling relieved that they’d arrived safely, a parcel came from home. I thought it was a thank-you gift, but when I opened it, it was the bowler hat. A letter was attached: Though you kindly procured this for me, it is a bit too large. Please take it to a hatter and have it shrunk. I shall send the cost of the alteration by money order. Indeed, he is quite obtuse, the master said, looking greatly satisfied to find someone in the world more obtuse than himself. And then what? There was nothing else to be done, so I took the hat and I'm wearing it myself. That hat? the master said, smirking. And is that gentleman a Baron? the mistress asked curiously. Who? That uncle with the iron fan. No, he's just a scholar of the Chinese classics. In his youth at the Seido academy, he became obsessed with Neo-Confucianism, so now he solemnly wears a topknot under electric lights. There's no helping him, Meitei said, stroking his chin. But you told that woman he was Baron Makiyama. You certainly did; I heard it from the dining room, the mistress said, agreeing with her husband. Did I? Ahahaha! Meitei laughed without reason. That was a lie. If I had a Baron for an uncle, I'd be a bureau chief by now, he said unconcernedly. I thought it sounded strange, the master said, looking both pleased and worried. Goodness, how can you tell such lies with such a straight face? You certainly are a master of tall tales, the mistress said, quite impressed. That woman is better at it than I am. You're no slouch yourself. But Ma'am, my tall tales are just tall tales. That woman’s lies all have a hidden motive and a purpose. They are of a malicious sort. If you confuse the tactics derived from a monkey’s wit with a divinely inspired sense of the comic, the God of Comedy will surely lament the lack of discerning eyes in this world. The master looked down and muttered, I wonder. The mistress laughed and said, It’s the same thing.

I have never set foot in the alley across the way. I had no idea what the Kaneda estate looked like, nor had I ever heard the name until now. Since businessmen are never mentioned in the master's house, even I, who eat the master's rice, was not only uninvolved with that world but entirely indifferent to it. However, after unexpectedly receiving a visit from Mrs. Nose, listening to her talk, imagining the beauty of her daughter, and thinking of their wealth and power, I found I could no longer lie idle on the veranda as a mere cat. Furthermore, I feel the deepest sympathy for Kangetsu-kun. While the other side has bought off the Doctor’s wife, the rickshaw man’s wife, and even the Lady Tenshoin of the two-stringed lute to spy on him—even discovering his broken front tooth without his knowledge—Kangetsu-kun just smirks and worries about his haori cord; for a newly graduated Bachelor of Science, he is far too lacking in skill. That said, dealing with a woman who has such a magnificent nose installed in her face is no easy task for anyone. The master is indifferent and far too poor to help in such matters. Meitei has no shortage of money, but being such an accidental acolyte, he would be of little use in aiding Kangetsu. Thus, the only one to be pitied is the teacher who lectures on the mechanics of hanging. It would be most unfair if I didn't make an effort to charge into the enemy fortress and scout the situation. I am a cat, but I am a cat residing in the house of a scholar who reads Epictetus and slams him onto the desk; I am of a different breed than the common, foolish cats of the world. The chivalry required to undertake this adventure is naturally tucked into the tip of my tail. It’s not that I owe Kangetsu-kun anything, but this is not merely a matter of youthful impulsiveness for an individual. To put it grandly, it is a glorious undertaking to realize the divine will that favors justice and loves the golden mean. Since they go about broadcasting the Azuma Bridge incident everywhere without permission; since they hide a dog under someone's eaves and then triumphantly boast about it to everyone they meet; since they use everyone from rickshaw men, grooms, ruffians, and vagrant students to charwomen, midwives, hags, masseurs, and simpletons to harass a man of use to the nation without a second thought—then a cat, too, must be prepared. Fortunately, the weather is fine. The melting frost is a bit of a nuisance, but I would give my life for the sake of the cause. If mud gets on my paws and leaves plum-blossom prints on the veranda, it might trouble the maid, but it is no pain to me. I shall not even wait until tomorrow; I shall go now. I was about to dash toward the kitchen with great resolve when I paused to think. As a cat, I have reached the height of evolution, and in terms of mental capacity, I believe I am no inferior to a third-year middle-school student; but alas, the structure of my throat is purely feline, and I cannot speak human language. Even if I successfully sneak into the Kaneda mansion and witness the enemy's situation, I cannot tell Kangetsu-kun. I cannot tell the master or Meitei. If I cannot speak, my knowledge would be like a diamond in the earth that cannot shine, a useless possession. This is foolish; perhaps I should stop, I thought, lingering at the entryway.

However, to abandon a plan once decided is somehow a matter of regret, like waiting for a rain shower only to see the black clouds pass into the neighboring province. If the fault were mine, it would be different, but for the sake of justice and humanity, a man of duty should press on even to a pointless death. Breaking my bones for nothing and getting my paws dirty is quite suitable for a cat. By the misfortune of being born a cat, I lack the skill to exchange thoughts with Kangetsu, Meitei, and Kushami via a three-inch tongue, but being a cat, I am more adept at the art of stealth than any of them. There is a pleasure in achieving what others cannot. Even if I am the only one, it is more pleasant to know the secrets of the Kaneda house than to know nothing. It is a pleasure just to give them the realization that they are being watched, even if I cannot tell anyone. With such pleasures following one after another, I simply must go. I shall go after all.

When I reached the alley across the way, the Western-style house I had heard of was occupying the corner lot with a proprietary air. Expecting the master of the house to be as arrogant as the building, I entered the gate and surveyed the architecture; but it was a structure of no merit, a two-story building standing pointlessly upright merely to intimidate people. This must be what Meitei calls commonplace. Keeping the entrance to my right, I passed through the shrubbery and went around to the kitchen door. The kitchen was indeed large, at least ten times the size of Kushami’s. It was so orderly and gleaming that I thought it no inferior to Count Okuma’s kitchen, which was described in detail in the Nippon newspaper recently. An exemplary kitchen, I thought, and slipped inside. In the earthen floor of about two tsubo, the rickshaw man’s wife was standing and talking animatedly to the cook and the groom. Thinking this was dangerous, I hid behind a water bucket. Does that teacher not know our master's name? the cook asked. How could he not? Anyone in this neighborhood who doesn't know the Kaneda estate is blind and deaf and a cripple, came the voice of the groom. You can't be sure. That teacher is an eccentric who knows nothing but books. He might be afraid if he knew anything about the master, but he’s hopeless; he doesn't even know the ages of his own children, the wife said. He doesn't even fear Mr. Kaneda? What a troublesome blockhead. It doesn't matter; why don't we all go and intimidate him? That’s a good idea. He says the mistress's nose is too large, that he doesn't like her face—he says such terrible things. Even though his own face looks like a tanuki made of Imado ware, he thinks he's a real person—it’s quite unbearable. It’s not just his face; the way he dangles a towel on his way to the bath is insufferably arrogant. He thinks there’s no one as great as himself, the cook said, showing that Kushami-sensei was very unpopular even with him. We should all go to his fence and shout insults at him. Then he'll surely be cowed. But it’s no fun if we show ourselves; just let him hear our voices, interrupt his studies, and provoke him as much as possible—the mistress gave those orders just now. I understand, the wife said, indicating she would take on a third of the insulting. So these are the ones who come to mock Kushami-sensei, I thought, as I slipped past the three of them and entered the inner rooms.

A cat’s feet are as if they weren't there; never has an clumsy sound been made wherever I walk. It is like treading on air, like traveling through clouds, like striking a gong underwater, like playing a lute in a deep cave. Like tasting the subtle flavor of the ultimate truth and knowing the temperature for oneself beyond the reach of words. There is no commonplace Western house, no exemplary kitchen, no rickshaw man’s wife, no groom, no cook, no young lady, no housemaid, no Mrs. Nose, and no master of the house. I simply go where I want to go, hear what I want to hear, stick out my tongue, wag my tail, prick up my whiskers, and return at my leisure. Furthermore, I am the most proficient in this art in all of Japan. I sometimes wonder if I inherit the blood of the cat-monsters in old picture books. They say a toad has a luminous pearl in its forehead, but in my tail is packed a secret medicine for mocking all the humans in the world, not to mention the matters of the gods, Buddhism, love, and impermanence. Walking through the Kaneda house unnoticed is easier for me than for a guardian deity to crush a piece of jelly. At that moment, I was impressed by my own power and realized it was all thanks to my tail, which I normally treat with such care. I thought I’d bow and pray to the Great Tail Deity for long-lasting feline luck, so I lowered my head for a moment, but it seemed my direction was a bit off. I had to face my tail and bow three times. When I turned my body to look at my tail, the tail naturally turned too. I tried to catch it by twisting my neck, but the tail kept the same distance and ran ahead. Indeed, being a spiritual object that contains the entire universe within three inches, it was more than I could handle; after circling around seven and a half times, I grew tired and stopped. I felt a bit dizzy. I lost my sense of direction for a moment. I didn't care and just walked around aimlessly. Behind a shoji, I heard Hanako's voice. I stopped there, pricked up my ears, and held my breath. Isn't he a conceited man for a poor teacher? she shrieked in her usual piercing voice. Yes, a conceited fellow. I’ll harass him a bit to teach him a lesson. There are people from my home province at that school. Who? Tsuki Pinsuke and Fukuchi Kishago are there; I’ll ask them to tease him. I don't know Mr. Kaneda's home province, but I was surprised that it was a place filled with people with such strange names. Kaneda continued, Is he an English teacher? Yes, the rickshaw man’s wife says he specializes in teaching English readers or something. He’s bound to be a mediocre teacher anyway. I encountered Pinsuke the other day and he said, There’s a strange fellow at my school. When a student asked him how to say "bancha" coarse tea in English, he seriously replied it was "Savage tea," and now he’s the laughingstock of the teachers; such a teacher is a nuisance to everyone else. It must be him. It’s definitely him; he has the face of someone who would say that, with that irritating mustache of his. An outrageous fellow. If having a mustache makes one outrageous, then there isn't a single cat that isn't outrageous. And that Meitei, or Hebereke or whatever he’s called—what a flighty, eccentric fellow he is! A Baron for an uncle? I knew a face like that couldn't possibly have a Baron for an uncle. It was your own fault for taking the word of someone of unknown origin seriously. Fault? He was far too mocking! she said, sounding very resentful. Strangely, not a single word was mentioned about Kangetsu-kun. Whether they had finished discussing him before I arrived, or whether he had already been failed and was no longer on their minds, I didn't know, but there was nothing to be done. I lingered for a while, then heard the sound of a bell from a room across the corridor. There was something going on over there too. I hurried in that direction before I missed anything.

When I arrived, I found a girl talking loudly to herself. Since her voice strongly resembled Hanako’s, I deduced that this must be the daughter who had caused Kangetsu-kun’s attempted drowning. What a pity I could not behold her jewel-like form through the shoji. Thus, I could not guarantee whether a large nose was enshrined in the middle of her face. However, judging from her manner of speaking and her heavy breathing, she didn't seem to have a modest pug nose that would go unnoticed. She was chattering away, yet the voice of the other person could not be heard at all; this must be the thing I’ve heard of called a telephone. Is that Yamato? Listen, I’m coming tomorrow. Get me a number three quail seat, you hear? Do you understand? What, you don't? Oh, don't be silly. Get a number three quail. What? You can't? You must be able to, just get it! Hehehe, you're joking? What’s a joke? You’re teasing me. Who are you, anyway? Nagakichi? I can't talk to a Nagakichi. Tell the mistress to come to the phone. What? You can handle anything? You’re insolent. Do you know who I am? I'm Kaneda. Hehehe, I know that well, you say? What a fool you are! I said I’m Kaneda! What? Thank you for your continued patronage? I don't want to hear your thanks! Oh, you’re laughing again. You really are an idiot. As you say, you say? If you keep making a fool of me, I’ll hang up. Is that all right? Won't you be in trouble? I can't tell if you're silent, say something! The person named Nagakichi must have hung up, for there was no reply. The young lady lost her temper and rang the bell frantically. A Pekingese at her feet was startled and began to bark. I couldn't afford to be careless, so I quickly jumped down and crawled under the floorboards.

Just then, footsteps approached in the corridor and a shoji was opened. Listening intently, I heard a voice that sounded like a lady's maid: Miss, the master and mistress are calling for you. I don't care, the daughter snapped. They said they have a bit of business and I should call you. How annoying, I said I don't care! the daughter snapped again. It seems to be about Mr. Mizushima Kangetsu... the maid said, trying to improve her mood. I don't care about Kangetsu or Suigetsu—I hate him! He has a face like a luffa that’s lost its way. Poor Kangetsu-kun received this third snap in his absence. Oh, when did you start doing your hair in that style? the maid sighed and said briefly, Today. How conceited of you, for a maid! came the fourth snap from another direction. And you've put on a new half-collar, haven't you? Yes, you gave it to me this New Year, Miss; I thought it was far too splendid and kept it in my trunk, but my old one was so dirty I changed it. When did I ever give you such a thing? This New Year, when you went to Shirokiya and bought it—it’s the greenish-brown one with the sumo wrestling program pattern. You said it was too plain for you and you’d give it to me. Oh, how disgusting. It suits you far too well. It’s hateful. I am honored. I wasn't praising you. It's hateful! Yes. Why did you take something that suits you so well without saying anything? Yes. And you wear it so coolly; how mean of you. The snaps came in rapid succession. I was listening to see how things would develop when Kaneda’s voice called loudly from the other room, Tomiko! Tomiko! The daughter had no choice but to reply, Yes, and left the telephone room. The Pekingese, which was a bit larger than me and had a face where its eyes and mouth seemed pulled toward the center, followed her. I returned to the street via the kitchen with my usual stealth and hurried back to the master's house. The expedition was a more than successful one.
