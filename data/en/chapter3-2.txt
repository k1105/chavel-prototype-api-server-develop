Finding myself unexpectedly appointed to entertain Master Meitei, I felt I couldn’t very well maintain a sour face, so I meowed charmingly and tried to crawl onto his lap. Meitei exclaimed, "Oho, you’ve gotten quite fat, haven't you? Let's see," and rudely grabbed me by the scruff of my neck, dangling me in mid-air. "Dangling your hind legs like that, you don't look like you could catch a single rat... Tell me, Ma'am, does this cat actually catch rats?" Not satisfied with just me, he called out to the mistress in the next room.

"Rats? Far from it. He eats New Year's ozoni and performs dances," she replied, exposing my past misdeeds from an unexpected quarter. Even while performing my mid-air acrobatics, I felt a bit embarrassed. Meitei still wouldn't put me down. "Indeed, he has the face for a dance. Ma'am, this cat has a physiognomy that bears watching. He resembles the 'cat-monsters' from old picture books," he said, talking incessantly to the mistress. Feeling pestered, she abandoned her needlework and came into the sitting room.

"I'm sorry to have left you bored. He should be back soon," she said, refilling Meitei's tea and setting it before him. "Where did he go, I wonder?" "He’s the sort of man who never says where he's going, so I can't be sure, but he’s likely gone to the doctor's." "Dr. Amagi? Poor Dr. Amagi, having to deal with such a patient is quite a misfortune." "Indeed," she replied briefly, seemingly at a loss for a better greeting. Meitei didn't mind at all. "How has he been lately? Is his stomach any better?" "I haven't the slightest idea if it's better or worse. No matter how much he sees Dr. Amagi, I don't think a stomach ailment will ever be cured if he licks nothing but jam all the time," she said, venting her earlier grievance to Meitei.

"Does he really lick that much jam? He’s just like a child," Meitei remarked. "It’s not just the jam; lately, he’s been licking grated radish incessantly, claiming it's medicine for his stomach..." "How amazing," Meitei marveled. "It’s because he read in a newspaper that there’s something called 'diastase' in grated radish." "I see. So his plan is to offset the damage from the jam. He’s quite the thinker, hahaha!" Meitei seemed delighted by her complaint. "The other day, he even made the baby lick some..." "Jam?" "No, grated radish. He said, 'Come here, my boy, your father has something delicious for you'—I thought for once he was showing affection to the children, but it was just more of his nonsense. And two or three days ago, he picked up our middle daughter and put her on top of the chest of drawers..." "What was the plan there?" Meitei asked, interpreting everything as a 'plan.' "There was no plan at all; he just told her to try jumping down from there. She’s only three or four years old; there’s no way she could do such a tomboyish thing." "Indeed, that was a plan lacking in substance. Still, at heart, he’s a good man with no malice." "If he had malice in his heart on top of all that, I simply couldn't endure it," she said with a burst of spirit.

"Now, there’s no need to complain so much. Living comfortably from day to day like this is quite enough. Kushami-kun is a man of simple tastes who doesn't gamble and doesn't care about clothes; he’s a solid, domestic man," Meitei said, giving an uncharacteristically cheerful sermon. "But you’re quite mistaken there..." "Does he have some secret hobby, then? It’s a deceptive world, after all," Meitei replied airily. "He has no other hobbies, but he buys an inordinate amount of books that he never even reads. It would be fine if he bought them with some discretion, but he goes to Maruzen whenever he pleases, takes whatever he wants, and then acts as if nothing happened when the bill comes at the end of the month. At the end of last year, the monthly balances had piled up and it was quite a struggle." "Oh, let him take all the books he wants. When they come for payment, just tell them you'll pay soon, and they'll go away." "Still, we can't keep putting them off forever," she said gloomily.

"In that case, talk some sense into him and get him to cut down the book budget." "How could I? No matter what I say, he never listens. The other day he told me, 'You are unfit to be the wife of a scholar; you don't understand the value of books at all. There is a story from ancient Rome on this matter. Listen to it for your future education.'" "That’s interesting. What story was it?" Meitei became eager, driven more by curiosity than sympathy. "Apparently, in ancient Rome, there was a king called Tar-kin..." "Tar-kin? That’s a bit strange." "I find foreign names difficult to remember. I think he said it was the seventh king." "I see, a seventh-generation Tar-kin is quite something. And what did this seventh Tar-kin do?" "Oh, if even you are going to tease me, I haven't a leg to stand on. If you know who it is, shouldn't you tell me? How mean of you," she snapped at Meitei. "Tease you? I’m not the sort of person to do such a mean thing. I just thought 'Seventh Tar-kin' had a nice ring to it... Now wait, the seventh king of Rome... I don't remember exactly, but it must be Tarquin the Proud. No matter who it was, what did this king do?"

"A woman came to this king with nine books and asked him to buy them. When the king asked how much, she named a terribly high price. Thinking it was too expensive, he asked if she wouldn't lower it a bit, and the woman suddenly threw three of the nine books into the fire and burned them. What a waste! They say the books contained prophecies and things that couldn't be found anywhere else. When the nine books became six, the king thought the price would surely decrease, but when he asked how much for the six, she named the same price as before and wouldn't take a single coin off. When he told her that was outrageous, she took three more and threw them into the fire. The king, seemingly still attached to them, asked how much for the remaining three, and she demanded the full price for all nine. Even though nine had become six, and six had become three, the price wouldn't drop a single cent. Fearing she might burn the last three if he tried to lower the price, the king finally paid a huge amount of money to buy the remaining three... My husband shouted at me, 'How about that? Surely this story has taught you the value of books!', but as for me, I still don't see what's so valuable about it," she said, asserting her own judgment and prompting Meitei's response.

Meitei, seemingly a bit at a loss, took a handkerchief from his sleeve and began to play with me. "But Ma'am," he said suddenly in a loud voice as if an idea had struck him, "it’s precisely because he buys all those books and stuffs his head with them that people call him a scholar and such. I saw a review of Kushami-kun in a literary magazine the other day." "Really?" she said, turning toward him. Her concern for her husband's reputation showed they were a typical couple. "What did it say?" "Oh, just two or three lines. It said that Kushami-kun's prose is 'like flowing clouds and running water.'" She smiled a bit and asked, "Is that all?" "The next part said: 'Just as it seems to appear, it suddenly vanishes; once it departs, it forgets to return for all eternity.'" She made a strange face and asked uncertainly, "Was that a compliment?" "I suppose you could call it a compliment," Meitei said coolly, dangling the handkerchief before my eyes. "Books are his tools of the trade, so I suppose it can't be helped, but he really is quite eccentric."

Meitei, thinking "Here she comes from another angle," offered a vague defense: "He is a bit eccentric, I suppose. All scholars are like that." "Just the other day, he came home from school and had to go right out again, but since changing his clothes was too much trouble, he sat at his desk and ate his dinner without even taking off his overcoat. He put his tray on top of the kotatsu frame—I was sitting there holding the rice container, and it was so ridiculous..." "It sounds rather like a dandy’s formal inspection of a severed head. But that’s precisely what makes Kushami-kun himself—at any rate, he isn't 'commonplace'," Meitei said, offering a strained compliment. "Whether it's commonplace or not, I wouldn't know, but no matter how you look at it, it’s far too reckless." "Still, it’s better than being commonplace."

She looked dissatisfied and asked point-blank: "You all use the word 'commonplace' all the time, but what exactly does it mean?" "Commonplace? Well—it’s a bit hard to explain..." "If it’s such an ambiguous thing, surely being commonplace shouldn't be a problem, then?" she pressed with a woman’s logic. "It’s not ambiguous at all; I understand it perfectly. It's just difficult to explain." "You just call anything you don't like 'commonplace,' don't you?" she remarked with unexpected insight. Meitei, forced to deal with the definition of "commonplace," tried to explain: "Ma'am, being 'commonplace' is—well, it's those people in their late twenties who, without a word, loll about in their daydreams, and as soon as the weather turns fine, they invariably grab a flask and go off to play at the Sumida embankment." "Are there really such people?" she asked, offering a non-committal greeting since she didn't understand. "It’s all so jumbled I don't follow."

"Well then, take the body of Bakin, put the head of Major Pendennis on it, and wrap it in the air of Europe for a year or two." "Would that produce something 'commonplace'?" Meitei didn't answer and just laughed. "You don't need to go to all that trouble. Just take a middle-school student, add a clerk from Shirokiya, and divide by two; that would produce a perfectly 'commonplace' specimen." "I suppose so," she said, tilting her head as if still not quite convinced.

"Are you still here?" the master asked, having returned unnoticed and sitting down beside Meitei. "That’s a bit harsh, isn't it? I told you I’d wait for you," Meitei replied. "Everything is like that with him," the mistress said, looking at Meitei. "While you were out, I heard all your anecdotes." "Women are far too talkative; if only humans would keep silent like this cat," the master said, stroking my head. "I hear you made the baby lick grated radish." "Hmph," the master laughed. "But babies these days are quite clever. Since then, whenever I ask where it hurts, he sticks out his tongue; it’s quite amusing." "It’s cruel, you’re treating him like a dog being trained to do tricks. By the way, Kangetsu should be here soon." "Kangetsu is coming?" the master asked suspiciously. "Yes. I sent him a postcard telling him to be at your house by one in the afternoon." "You’re a man who does exactly as he pleases without consulting others. What are you calling Kangetsu for?" "Oh, today’s event isn't my plan; it’s Kangetsu-sensei's own request. He’s supposed to give a lecture at the Physical Science Association. He asked me to listen to his rehearsal, and I thought it would be a perfect chance for Kushami to hear it too. So I decided to have him come to your house—besides, you have plenty of time, so it’s perfect—you’re not a man with any pressing engagements, so you might as well listen," Meitei said, having decided everything on his own.

"I won't understand a lecture on physics," the master said, as if resenting Meitei's high-handedness. "But the topic isn't some dry, flavorless thing like magnetized nozzles. It’s an extraordinary, transcendental subject: $\text{The Mechanics of Hanging}$. It’s worth a listen." "Since you’re the man who failed to hang himself, you should listen well, but as for me..." "A man who gets a chill at the Kabuki-za shouldn't conclude he can't listen to this," Meitei quipped as usual. The mistress gave a giggle and retired to the next room, looking back at her husband. The master silently stroked my head. This time, his stroking was exceptionally gentle.
