Four or five days passed without anything of particular note. I spent my time watching the white porcelain narcissus gradually wither and the blue-stemmed plum blossoms in the vase slowly begin to open, but finding this dull, I attempted to visit Mike-ko once or twice, though I could not see her. At first, I thought she was simply out, but on my second visit, I learned she was bedridden with an illness. Hiding behind the shade of the Haran leaves by the washbasin, I overheard the lute teacher and the maid talking behind the shoji.

"Is Mike eating anything?"

"No, she hasn't eaten a thing since this morning. I’ve kept her warm and let her sleep in the kotatsu." How unlike a cat; she was being treated exactly like a human. On one hand, I was envious compared to my own situation, but on the other, I was glad that the cat I loved was receiving such kind treatment.

"It’s quite a worry. If she doesn't eat, her body will only grow weaker."

"Indeed it will. Even we can't work the next day if we don't have our meals for a single day." The maid spoke as if the cat were a superior animal to herself. In truth, in this house, the cat might be more important than the maid.

"Did you take her to the doctor?"

"Yes, but that doctor was quite strange. When I took Mike to the examination room in my arms, he tried to take my pulse, asking if I’d caught a cold! I told him, 'No, I'm not the patient, it's this one,' and I set Mike on his lap. He just smirked and said he didn't understand cat illnesses and that she’d probably get better if left alone. Wasn't that too cruel? I got so angry I told him I didn't need his help even if he offered it, and that this was a very precious cat, then I tucked Mike into my robe and came straight home."

"Truly, indeed."

"Truly, indeed"—these are words one never hears in my house. It seems they can only be used by someone connected to the secretary of Lady Tenshoin; I was impressed by how elegant it sounded.

"She seems to be whimpering a bit..."

"Yes, she surely has a cold and her throat must be sore. When one catches a cold, anyone will cough, after all..." The maid used excessively polite language, befitting a servant of Lady Tenshoin’s "something-or-other."

"And lately there is this thing called 'consumption' going around..."

"Truly, these days with consumption and the plague and all these new diseases increasing, one can't let one's guard down for a moment."

"Nothing that didn't exist in the old Shogunate days is any good, so you must be careful too."

"I suppose you're right." The maid seemed greatly moved.

"Even though she has a cold, she didn't seem to go out much..."

"Oh no, Ma'am, you see, she’s made a 'bad friend' lately." The maid looked quite triumphant, as if revealing a state secret.

"A bad friend?"

"Yes, that dingy tomcat belonging to the teacher on the main street."

"The teacher? You mean the man who makes those unrefined noises every morning?"

"Yes, the one who sounds like a goose being strangled every time he washes his face."

"A goose being strangled"—that was an apt description. Every morning in the bathroom, my master has a habit of poking his throat with a toothpick while gargling and letting out strange, loud noises without any reservation. When he is in a bad mood, he does it desperately; when he is in a good mood, he does it with vigor. In short, he does it incessantly whether his mood is good or bad. According to the mistress, he didn't have this habit before they moved here, but since he suddenly started one day, he hasn't missed a single morning. It is a troublesome habit, and why he continues it so persistently is beyond the imagination of a cat. That aside, "dingy cat" was a harsh criticism; I pricked up my ears further to listen.

"I wonder what kind of spell he thinks he's casting with that voice. Before the Restoration, even grooms and sandal-bearers knew proper manners; no one in a residential district would wash their face like that."

"You're quite right, Ma'am." The maid was immensely impressed and kept using her "indeed"s.

"A cat with a master like that is bound to be a stray at heart anyway. If he comes again, give him a bit of a beating."

"Oh, I'll beat him, alright. I've no doubt it's entirely his fault that Mike fell ill. I'll certainly get revenge for her."

I had been subjected to a terrible false accusation. Deciding this was no place to linger, I returned home without meeting Mike-ko after all.

Back at home, the master was in his study, holding a brush with a look of deep contemplation. If I told him the reputation he had at the lute teacher’s house, he would surely be angry, but "ignorance is bliss," as they say; he was groaning to himself, fully immersed in the role of a sacred poet.

Just then, Meitei-kun—who had sent a New Year's card saying he was too busy to visit—suddenly drifted in.

"Are you composing some new-style poetry? Show me if you've done anything interesting," he said.

"Well, I came across some fine prose, so I thought I'd try translating it," the master said, opening his mouth heavily.

"Prose? By whom?"

"I don't know whose it is."

"Anonymous? Even anonymous works can be quite good; one can't look down on them. Where did you find it?"

"The Second Reader," the master replied with total composure.

"The Second Reader? What about it?"

"I mean the masterpiece I'm translating is in the Second Reader."

"You're joking. Is this your way of getting a narrow revenge for the peacock tongues?"

"I am not a blower of tall tales like you," the master said, twisting his mustache with an air of serenity.

"There is a story that when someone asked Sanyo if there were any fine prose pieces lately, he showed them a debt-collection letter written by a stableman and said, 'This is probably the finest prose of late.' So perhaps your aesthetic sense is unexpectedly reliable. Go on, read it to me; I'll critique it for you," Meitei-san said, as if he were the head of the house of aesthetics. The master began to read in a voice like a Zen monk reciting the final admonitions of Daito Kokushi.

"The Giant, Gravity."

"What’s that? 'The Giant Gravity'?"

"That's the title."

"A strange title. I don't follow the meaning."

"It means a giant who has the name 'Gravity'."

"A bit of a stretch, but since it's a title, I'll let it pass. Now, hurry up and read the text; you have a good voice, so it should be interesting."

"Don't interrupt," the master cautioned before starting again.

Kate looks out the window. Children are playing, throwing a ball. They toss the ball high into the air. The ball goes up and up. After a while, it falls back down. They toss the ball high again. A second time, a third. Every time they toss it, the ball falls back. 'Why does it fall? Why does it not just go up and up?' Kate asks. 'Because a giant lives in the earth,' her mother replies. 'He is the Giant Gravity. He is strong. He pulls all things toward him. He pulls houses to the ground. If he did not, they would fly away. The children would fly away, too. You have seen the leaves fall. That is the Giant Gravity calling them. You may drop a book. That is because the Giant Gravity says "Come." The ball goes up into the sky. The Giant Gravity calls. When he calls, it falls back down.'

"Is that all?"

"Yes. Isn't it exquisite?"

"Well, I am impressed. I've received a magnificent counter-gift for the Tochimenbo."

"It's not a counter-gift or anything else. I translated it because it truly is fine writing. Don't you think so?" the master said, looking into the depths of the gold-rimmed glasses.

"I'm quite amazed. To think you had such talent; I've been completely hoodwinked this time. I surrender, I surrender," Meitei said, agreeing and chatting away all by himself. His words didn't reach the master at all.

"I had no intention of making you surrender. I simply translated it because I thought it was an interesting piece."

"No, it really is interesting. It wouldn't be the real thing otherwise. It's formidable. I'm humbled."

"There's no need to be so humbled. I’ve given up watercolors lately, so I thought I’d try my hand at writing instead."

"Indeed, it's not even in the same league as those 'distance-blind, black-and-white-equal' watercolors. I'm deeply impressed."

"If you praise me so much, I'll start feeling motivated," the master said, completely misunderstanding the situation.

Just then, Kangetsu-kun entered, saying, "Excuse me for the other day."

"Ah, forgive us. We were just listening to a tremendous masterpiece and exorcising the ghost of the Tochimenbo," Meitei-san said, hinting at things that made no sense.

"Ah, I see," Kangetsu replied with an equally nonsensical greeting. Only the master showed no sign of being particularly elated.

"The other day, a man named Ochi Kochi came to see me, introduced by you."

"Oh, did he come? That man, Ochi Kochi, is extremely honest, but he has some eccentricities, so I wondered if he might be a nuisance. But he insisted on being introduced..."

"He wasn't a particular nuisance..."

"Did he happen to lecture you about his own name while he was here?"

"No, there was no such talk."

"Is that so? He has a habit of explaining his name to everyone he meets for the first time."

"What sort of explanation?" Meitei-kun chimed in, scenting a story.

"He gets very upset if people read 'Kochi' with the Chinese-style pronunciation 'To-fu'."

"I see," Meitei-san said, taking tobacco from his gold-leather case.

"He always insists: 'My name is not Ochi To-fu; it is Ochi Kochi (Here).' "

"How strange," Meitei said, inhaling the 'Kumoi' smoke deep into his lungs.

"It all comes from his passion for literature. He prides himself on the fact that when read as 'Kochi,' it forms the phrase 'En-ko' (Far and Near), and furthermore, that his name and surname rhyme. That's why he complains that if people read it as 'To-fu,' they are failing to appreciate his hard work."

"That is indeed eccentric," Meitei-san said, getting into the spirit and exhaling the smoke from the depths of his belly out through his nostrils. Midway, the smoke lost its way and got caught in the exit of his throat. He clutched his pipe and coughed and choked.

"When he came the other day, he mentioned that he played the boatman at a recitation meeting and was laughed at by schoolgirls," the master said with a laugh.

"Aha! That's the one!" Meitei-san slapped his knee with his pipe. I moved a bit further away, sensing danger. "That recitation society. When I treated him to Tochimenbo the other day, that came up. Apparently, for the second meeting, they plan to invite famous literati and hold a grand convention, and he insisted that I must attend. Then, when I asked if he was going to do another Chikamatsu domestic drama, he said, 'No, next time we’ve chosen something much newer: The Golden Demon.' When I asked what role he had, he said, 'I am O-miya.' Kochi as O-miya should be a hoot. I’m definitely going to attend and cheer him on."

"That should be interesting," Kangetsu-kun said with a strange laugh.

"But that man is good because he is sincere to the core and lacks any flippancy. He’s quite different from the likes of Meitei," the master said, taking his revenge for Andrea del Sarto, the peacock tongues, and the Tochimenbo all at once.

Meitei-kun, seemingly unbothered, laughed and said, "Well, I suppose I'm just like a 'chopping board of Gyotoku' then."

"I suppose you are," the master said. In truth, the master didn't understand the phrase "chopping board of Gyotoku," but having bluffed his way through years as a teacher, he applied his classroom experience to his social life.

"What does 'chopping board of Gyotoku' mean?" Kangetsu asked innocently.

The master looked toward the alcove. "Those narcissuses—I bought them and arranged them on my way back from the bath at the end of the year. They've lasted quite well, haven't they?" He forced the subject of the Gyotoku chopping board into submission.

"Speaking of the end of the year, I had a truly strange experience last December," Meitei said, twirling his pipe on his fingertip like a circus performer.

"What sort of experience? Do tell," the master said, feeling relieved that he had left the Gyotoku chopping board far behind. Meitei-san’s strange experience was as follows.

"I believe it was the twenty-seventh of December. Since Kochi had sent word that he would be visiting to hear some 'noble talk on literature' and asked me to be at home, I waited expectantly from the morning, but the man didn't show up. I had finished lunch and was reading Barry Pain’s humor in front of the stove when a letter arrived from my mother in Shizuoka. Being elderly, she still thinks of me as a child. She gave all sorts of warnings: not to go out at night during the midwinter, and that while cold baths are fine, I’d catch a cold if I didn't keep the room warm with the stove. Truly, a parent’s love is a precious thing; no one else would go that far. Even a carefree fellow like me was deeply moved at that moment. And considering that, I felt it was a waste to spend my days idling about. I felt I must achieve some great work and bring honor to the family name. I felt I wanted to let the world know of the existence of Master Meitei in the Meiji literary world while my mother was still alive.

Then, as I read on, she wrote that I am a very lucky person. While war with Russia has begun and young people are working terribly hard and suffering for their country, I am playing around as lightheartedly as if the year-end were the New Year. —I’m not playing around as much as she thinks!— Following that, she listed the names of my primary school friends who had died or been wounded in the war. Reading each name, the world somehow felt desolate and I felt that human life was trivial. At the very end, she wrote: 'As I am reaching such an age, this may be the last time I celebrate the New Year’s ozoni...' Writing such depressing things made me feel even more gloomy, and I wished Kochi would hurry up and arrive, but the man never came.

Eventually, it was time for dinner, so I thought I’d write a reply to my mother and jotted down about twelve or thirteen lines. My mother’s letters are over six feet long, but I don't have that kind of talent, so I’ve made it a rule to be excused with about ten lines or so. Then, having stayed still all day, my stomach felt strange and uncomfortable. Thinking I’d let Kochi wait if he came, I decided to go for a walk while I went to post the letter. Contrary to my habit, my feet didn't turn toward Fujimi-cho, but found themselves heading toward Dote-Sanbancho. That night happened to be a bit cloudy, and a dry wind blew from across the moat; it was extremely cold. A train from the direction of Kagurazaka went whew and passed below the embankment. It felt terribly lonely. Thoughts of the year-end, death in battle, old age, and the swiftness of impermanence whirled around in my head. People often say they’ll hang themselves, and I wondered if they are suddenly enticed into wanting to die at such a moment. Raising my head slightly to look atop the embankment, I found I had arrived, before I knew it, right under that pine tree."

"What 'that' pine tree?" the master interjected.

"The Hanging Pine," Meitei said, shrinking his neck.

"The Hanging Pine is at Konodai, isn't it?" Kangetsu added to the ripple.

"The one at Konodai is the 'Bell-Hanging Pine'; the one at Dote-Sanbancho is the 'Neck-Hanging Pine'. As for why it has that name, tradition says that anyone who comes under this pine feels like hanging themselves. There are dozens of pines on the embankment, but if someone shouts 'There’s a hanging!', it’s always from this tree. It happens two or three times every year. They simply don't feel like dying on any other tree. Looking up, I saw a branch conveniently sticking out horizontally over the path. Ah, what a fine branch. It would be a waste to leave it as it is. I thought, 'I’d like to see a human dangling from there somehow; I wonder if anyone will come along,' but unfortunately, no one came. I thought, 'There's nothing for it; shall I hang myself?' But no, if I hang myself, I’ll lose my life, so I’d better not. However, there’s a story that ancient Greeks used to add to the entertainment at banquets by pretending to hang themselves. One person would climb onto a platform and put their neck into a noose, and at that moment, another would kick the platform away. The one who had their neck in the noose would jump down the moment the platform was pulled away, loosening the rope. If that’s true, then there’s nothing to fear; I thought I’d give it a try. When I laid my hands on the branch, it bent quite nicely. The way it flexed was truly aesthetic. Imagining the feeling of my neck being caught and bobbing up and down, I was overcome with joy. I decided I simply must do it, but then I remembered Kochi might be waiting and felt it would be a pity. So I thought I’d first meet Kochi, talk to him as promised, and then come back later, and I finally went home."

"And did that help the city prosper?" the master asked.

"That’s an interesting story," Kangetsu said with a smirk.

"When I got home, Kochi wasn't there. Instead, there was a postcard saying he couldn't come today due to unavoidable circumstances and hoped to meet me on a longer day. I felt finally relieved, thinking, 'Good, now I can hang myself without any worries!' So I immediately slipped on my sandals and hurried back to the original spot..." He looked at the master and Kangetsu with a smug face.

"And what did you see?" the master asked, getting a bit impatient.

"We're reaching the best part," Kangetsu said, fiddling with his haori cord.

"When I looked, someone had already come and was dangling there ahead of me! I missed it by just a single step—it was a great shame, my friends. Come to think of it, I must have been possessed by a death god at that time. As James might say, the underworld of the subconscious and the real world where I exist must have resonated with each other through some law of causality. Truly, strange things do happen, don't they?" Meitei said with total composure.

The master, thinking he’d been had again, said nothing and stuffed a Kuya-mochi into his mouth, his mouth working busily.

Kangetsu, having been carefully smoothing the ash in the brazier and smirking with his head down, finally spoke. His tone was extremely quiet.

"Indeed, hearing that, it is a strange thing and hardly seems possible, but since I myself had a similar experience just the other day, I am not inclined to doubt it at all."

"Oh? Did you feel like hanging yourself too?"

"No, mine wasn't the neck. This happened just at the end of last year, and on the very same day and at the same hour as yours, Sensei, which makes it seem even more mysterious."

"This is getting interesting," Meitei said, also stuffing a Kuya-mochi into his mouth.

"That day, there was a year-end party and concert at an acquaintance's house in Mukojima, and I went there with my violin. There were fifteen or sixteen young ladies and matrons gathered, and it was a grand occasion; everything was so well-arranged that I thought it a rare pleasure. Dinner was over, the concert was finished, and as the talk went on and the hour grew late, I was thinking of saying my goodbyes and leaving when the wife of a certain Doctor came to my side and asked in a low voice: 'Are you aware of OO-ko-san's illness?' Since she had seemed perfectly healthy when I met her two or three days earlier, I was surprised and asked for details. It seems she had suddenly developed a fever the night I saw her and had been delirious incessantly. That in itself would be bad enough, but in her delirium, my name was apparently mentioned from time to time."

The master and Meitei-san, of course, did not use such commonplaces as "How romantic!", but listened in solemn silence.

"When they called a doctor, he said he didn't know the name of the illness, but since the fever was so intense it was affecting the brain, it would be dangerous if the sedatives didn't work as expected. As soon as I heard that, a sort of unpleasant feeling arose within me. It was a heavy, stifling feeling, like being oppressed in a nightmare, and I felt as if the surrounding air had suddenly become solid and was tightening around me from all sides. On my way back, my head was full of nothing but that, and it was unbearably painful. That beautiful, that cheerful, that healthy OO-ko-san..."

"Excuse me, but wait a moment. I've heard you mention 'OO-ko-san' twice now; if it's not a problem, I’d like to know who she is," the master said, looking toward the guest, who gave a noncommittal "Mmm."

"No, that might cause her trouble, so let's leave it aside."

"Do you intend to proceed with everything in this vague and murky fashion?"

"Don't sneer; this is a very serious story... In any case, when I thought of that lady suddenly falling ill like that, my heart was filled with thoughts of 'falling blossoms and drifting leaves,' and my vitality seemed to go on strike all at once; my spirits sank, and I found myself stumbling and reeling toward Azuma Bridge. Leaning against the railing and looking down—I don't know if it was high tide or low tide—the black water seemed to be a solid mass just moving along. A rickshaw came running from Hanakawado and passed over the bridge. Watching the light of its lantern, it grew smaller and smaller until it vanished near the Sapporo Beer factory. I looked at the water again. Then, from far upstream, I heard a voice calling my name. I thought, 'That’s strange; there’s no reason for anyone to call me at this hour,' and I peered at the surface of the water, but it was too dark to see anything. Thinking it must be my imagination, I took a step or two to leave, when I heard my name called again by a faint voice from far away. I stopped again and pricked up my ears. When I was called a third time, my knees began to shake even as I clutched the railing. That voice seemed to come from far away, or from the bottom of the river, but it was unmistakably the voice of OO-ko. Unconsciously, I replied, 'Yes!' My voice was so loud it echoed over the quiet water, and startled by my own voice, I looked around. Not a person, a dog, or even the moon was to be seen. At that moment, I was overcome by a sudden urge to be swept up into this 'night' and go to where that voice was coming from. OO-ko's voice pierced my ears again, sounding pained, as if appealing for help, as if seeking rescue; this time I replied, 'I’m coming right now!', leaned halfway over the railing, and gazed at the black water. I felt as if the voice calling me was forcing its way up from beneath the waves. Thinking 'It’s under this water,' I finally climbed onto the railing. I resolved to jump the next time she called, and as I stared at the current, a pitiful voice floated up like a thread. Thinking 'Now!', I gave a mighty leap and fell, without any lingering attachment, like a pebble or something."

"Did you finally jump in?" the master asked, eyes wide.

"I didn't think you’d go that far," Meitei said, pinching the tip of his nose.

"After I jumped, I lost consciousness and was in a daze for a while. When I eventually came to, I felt cold, but I wasn't wet anywhere, nor did I feel like I’d swallowed any water. I was sure I’d jumped, so it was truly mysterious. Thinking it was strange, I looked around and was quite surprised. I had intended to jump into the water, but I had accidentally jumped down onto the middle of the bridge instead; it was a great disappointment. Just because of a mistake between 'front' and 'back,' I wasn't able to go to where that voice was coming from." Kangetsu smirked and, as usual, busied himself with his haori cord.

"Hahaha! That's wonderful. The fact that it's so similar to my experience is what's so curious. It really is material for Professor James. If you wrote a sketch with the title 'Human Resonance,' it would surely shock the literary world... And what happened to OO-ko-san's illness?" Meitei-san pursued.

"When I went for a New Year's visit two or three days ago, she was playing battledore and shuttlecock with the maid inside the gate, so it seems her illness has completely cleared up."

The master had been in a state of deep thought for some time, but finally he spoke, showing his competitive spirit: "I have one, too."

"You? What do you have?" In Meitei's eyes, the master was of no account.

"Mine was also at the end of last year."

"Everyone’s having coincidences at the end of last year; how mysterious," Kangetsu laughed. Some Kuya-mochi was stuck in his missing front tooth.

"Was it on the same day and at the same hour too?" Meitei chimed in.

"No, the day seems to be different. It was around the twentieth. My wife asked if I’d take her to hear Settsu-daijo as a year-end treat. I thought I might as well, but when I asked what the play was for today, she consulted the newspaper and said it was 'Unagidani.' I dislike 'Unagidani,' so I told her we’d skip it and gave it up for that day. The next day, she brought the newspaper again and said, 'It’s "Horikawa" today, so that should be fine.' I said 'Horikawa' is just noisy with the shamisen and has no substance, so we’d skip that too, and she withdrew with a dissatisfied face. The next day, she said, 'It’s "Sanjusangendo" today. I simply must hear Settsu’s "Sanjusangendo." I don't know if you dislike "Sanjusangendo" too, but since you're taking me to hear it, surely you can come with me,' making it a final ultimatum. I told her, 'If you want to go that badly, very well. But since it’s his final performance of a lifetime, it will be incredibly crowded; we’ll never get in if we just show up. Normally, when you go to such places, there are teahouses, and you’re supposed to negotiate with them to reserve proper seats; it’s not right to act outside the norms by skipping that step. It’s a pity, but let’s give it up for today.' Then she gave me a fierce look and said, 'I'm a woman, so I don't know anything about such difficult procedures, but Mother from Ohara and Kimiyo Suzuki went and heard it perfectly well without all that. No matter if you're a teacher, surely you can see a show without such a fuss. You’re being too much!', and her voice sounded like she was about to cry. 'All right, then, even if we can't get in, let's go. We'll have dinner and go by tram,' I surrendered. Then she suddenly became energetic: 'If we're going, we must be there by four o'clock. We can't keep dallying around like this!' When I asked why we had to be there by four, she stated exactly what Kimiyo Suzuki had told her: 'Because if you don't go that early and secure a place, you won't get in.' I double-checked: 'So if it's past four, it's no good?' and she replied, 'Yes, it's no good.' Then, you see—and this is a strange thing—at that moment, I suddenly came down with a chill."

"Did your wife?" Kangetsu asked.

"No, she was as fit as a fiddle. I did. Just as I felt a sensation of shrinking all at once like a punctured balloon, I became dizzy and couldn't move."

"A sudden illness," Meitei commented.

"Ah, what a fix. Since it was my wife's once-a-year wish, I really wanted to fulfill it. Usually I just scold her, or don't speak to her, or make her struggle with our finances and the children; I have never once rewarded her for her hard work in the house. Today I fortunately have the time, and I have four or five coins in my pocket. If I take her, we can go. She surely wants to go, and I want to take her. I truly want to take her, but with such a chill and this dizziness, I can't even step down to the entryway, let alone get on a tram. Thinking 'What a pity, what a pity,' the chill and the dizziness only grew worse. I thought if I saw a doctor quickly and took some medicine, I’d be better by four, so I consulted with my wife and sent for Dr. Amagi, but unfortunately he had been on duty last night and hadn't returned from the university yet. The message was that he would be back around two and would come over as soon as he returned. What a nuisance. If I could just drink some almond water now, I’d surely be better by four, but in times of bad luck, nothing goes as one hopes; my plan to enjoy seeing my wife’s happy face was about to fall through completely. My wife asked with a reproachful face, 'Can you really not go?' 'I’ll go, I’ll certainly go. I’ll make sure I'm better by four, so don't worry. Go wash your face and change your clothes and wait for me,' I said, but my heart was filled with infinite emotion. The chill became even more intense, and my head whirled more and more. If I couldn't recover by four and fulfill my promise, being a narrow-minded woman, there was no telling what she might do. It was a miserable situation. What should I do? Thinking of the worst, I wondered if it wasn't my duty as a husband to teach her the principle of 'all things are transient' and the path of 'all living things must die,' so that she would be prepared and not lose her composure if something should happen. I quickly called her into the study. I told her, 'You are a woman, but surely you know the Western proverb "many a slip 'twixt the cup and the lip".' She snapped back, 'Who knows such foreign words? You know perfectly well I don't know English, yet you use it on purpose to tease me. Fine then, I don't know English anyway, so if you like it so much, why didn't you marry a graduate of a mission school? There’s no one as cold-hearted as you!' and she was so furious that my well-laid plan was ruined. I'll explain to you both: I didn't use English out of any ill intent. It came entirely from a deep affection for my wife, and it’s a bit much for me if she interprets it that way. Besides, my brain was a bit muddled from the chill and dizziness, and I was a bit rushed to make her understand the transience of life, so I simply forgot that she didn't know English and used it without thinking. Looking back, I was at fault; it was a total oversight. With this failure, the chill became even stronger. My head whirled even more. My wife, as ordered, went to the bathroom, washed herself, took her clothes out of the chest, and changed. She was waiting as if she could leave at any moment. I was on pins and needles. Wishing Dr. Amagi would hurry up and arrive, I looked at the clock and saw it was already three. There was only one hour left until four. 'Shall we be leaving soon?' my wife asked, peeking her head through the study door. It feels strange to praise my own wife, but I never thought she looked as beautiful as she did then. Her skin, polished with soap, was glowing and stood out against her black crepe haori. Her face shone from both physical and spiritual aspects—from the soap and from her hope of hearing Settsu-daijo. I felt I must satisfy that hope and take her. 'Well then, shall I make an effort and go?' I thought, and as I was puffing on a pipe, Dr. Amagi finally arrived. Everything went according to my wishes. But as I described my condition, Dr. Amagi looked at my tongue, took my hand, tapped my chest, rubbed my back, turned my eyelids inside out, felt my skull, and brooded for a while. 'I feel it's a bit dangerous,' I said. The doctor replied calmly, 'No, there's nothing particularly wrong.' My wife asked, 'It won't be a problem if he goes out for a bit, will it?' 'Well...' the doctor brooded again. 'If he doesn't feel too bad...' 'I do feel bad,' I said. 'Then I'll give you a dose of medicine and some liquid medicine anyway.' 'I see. It feels like things might turn critical.' 'No, it's nothing to worry about; you mustn't let it get to your nerves,' the doctor said and left. It was half past three. We sent the maid to get the medicine. Under my wife's strict orders, she ran there and ran back. It was fifteen minutes before four. There were still fifteen minutes until four. Then—and this is truly mysterious—from about fifteen minutes before four, although I had been fine until then, I suddenly felt an urge to vomit. My wife poured the liquid medicine into a cup and set it before me. When I took the cup and tried to drink, something shouted from inside my stomach: 'Gah!' I had to put the cup down. My wife pressed me: 'You should drink it quickly!' It would be a breach of faith if I didn't drink it quickly and leave. Resolving to just drink it, I put the cup to my lips again, but the 'Gah' persistently blocked me. I tried to drink and put the cup down, tried to drink and put it down, and then the wall clock in the dining room went ting-ting-ting-ting and struck four. 'Right, it's four o'clock. No time for dallying!', and as I took up the cup again—it was truly strange, my friends, truly mysterious—with the sound of the four o'clock bell, the nausea completely stopped, and I was able to drink the medicine without any trouble. Then, around ten past four, I finally began to understand that Dr. Amagi is a master physician; the shivers in my back and the whirling in my head vanished like a dream, and I was delighted to find that the illness, which I thought would keep me from standing for some time, had completely vanished."

"And did you go to the Kabuki-za together then?" Meitei asked with a face that showed he missed the point.

"I wanted to go, but since it was past four, my wife's opinion was that we couldn't get in, so there was nothing for it; we gave it up. If Dr. Amagi had come just fifteen minutes earlier, my honor would have been saved and my wife would have been satisfied. For a mere fifteen-minute difference, it was a great shame. Even now, thinking back, I feel it was a close call."

Having finished his story, the master looked as if he had finally fulfilled his duty. Perhaps he felt his honor was now upheld before the two of them.

Kangetsu, as usual showing his missing tooth and laughing, said, "That was a shame indeed."

Meitei said with a blank face, as if talking to himself, "A wife with such a kind husband is truly lucky." From behind the shoji, a cough from the mistress—A-hem—was heard.

I listened quietly to the talk of the three in turn, but I felt neither amused nor sad. I thought humans are beings with no other talent than to move their mouths for the sake of killing time, laughing at things that aren't funny and being happy over things that aren't interesting. I had known of my master's selfishness and narrow-mindedness from before, but since he usually doesn't say much, I had felt there was some point I couldn't quite understand. In that incomprehensible point, I had felt a bit of fear, but after hearing his story just now, I suddenly felt like despising him. Why couldn't he listen to the talk of the other two in silence? What did he gain by being competitive and spouting such nonsensical drivel? I wonder if Epictetus tells him to do such things. In short, the master, Kangetsu, and Meitei are all "idlers of peace," and while they seem to be as aloof as a sponge-gourd blowing in the wind, in reality, they still have worldly attachments and greed. A competitive spirit, a desire to win, win, win, flickers through their daily chatter, and I feel it is most pitiful, from a cat's perspective, that they are animals of the same stripe as the "vulgar bones" they usually denounce. Only the fact that their words and actions lack the stereotyped unpleasantness of the average half-educated person might be a small saving grace.

Thinking thus, the conversation of the three suddenly lost its interest, so I went around to the garden entrance of the lute teacher to see how Mike-ko was doing. The gate pines and the festive decorations had already been removed, and it was now the tenth of January. From a deep sky without a single cloud, a calm spring sun shone over the whole world at once, and even the garden, though less than ten tsubo, showed a more vivid vitality than when it received the first light of New Year’s Day. There was a cushion on the veranda but no sign of anyone, and the shoji were all closed; I wondered if the teacher had gone to the bath. It didn't matter if the teacher was out, but I was concerned whether Mike-ko was doing any better. Since it was quiet and there was no sign of anyone, I climbed onto the veranda with my muddy feet and curled up in the middle of the cushion; it felt wonderful. I drifted off into a doze, forgetting all about Mike-ko, when suddenly I heard voices behind the shoji.

"Good work. Is it finished?" The teacher had not been out after all.

"Yes, I'm sorry to be late. When I went to the Buddhist carver's, he said it had just been finished."

"Let me see. Ah, it's beautifully done. Now Mike's soul can rest in peace. The gold won't peel off, will it?"

"Yes, I made sure of that. He said he used high-quality gold, so it will last longer than a human memorial tablet... And he said that for the character 'Hyo' in 'Nekohyo Shinnyo,' a cursive style would look better, so he changed the strokes slightly."

"Let me see, let me see. Let's put it on the altar right away and light some incense."

"Has something happened to Mike-ko?" I thought, standing up on the cushion, feeling that something was wrong. Ting! "Namu Nekohyo Shinnyo, Namu Amida Butsu, Namu Amida Butsu," came the teacher's voice.

"You should offer a prayer too."

Ting! "Namu Nekohyo Shinnyo, Namu Amida Butsu, Namu Amida Butsu," came the maid's voice this time. My heart suddenly began to throb. Standing on the cushion, I remained as motionless as a wooden cat, not even my eyes moving.

"It truly was a shame. It probably started as just a little cold..."

"Perhaps it would have been better if Dr. Amagi had given her some medicine."

"In fact, it's that Dr. Amagi's fault. He looked down on Mike too much."

"Now, one shouldn't speak ill of others. This too was her allotted span of life."

It seemed Mike-ko had also been examined by Dr. Amagi.

"In the end, I think it's because that stray cat at the teacher's house on the main street kept enticing her out."

"Yes, that beast is Mike's enemy," the maid said. I wanted to make some excuse, but I held my breath and listened, knowing this was a time for patience. The talk stopped for a moment.

"The world doesn't go as one wishes. A beauty like Mike dies young, while ugly stray cats are healthy and up to no good..."

"That's exactly right. A lovely cat like Mike couldn't be found twice, even if you searched with a bell and a drum."

She said "two people" instead of "two cats." In the maid's mind, cats and humans seemed to be of the same species. Come to think of it, the maid's face bore a striking resemblance to our cat species.

"If only, in Mike's place..."

"It would have been just as ordered if that stray at the teacher's house had died instead."

If things went "as ordered" in that way, it would be a bit of a problem for me. As for what dying is like, I haven't experienced it yet, so I can't say whether I like it or dislike it. But the other day, because it was so cold, I crawled into a charcoal-extinguishing jar, and the maid, not knowing I was there, put the lid on. The agony of that moment was terrifying even to think about. According to Shiro-kun's explanation, if that agony had continued a bit longer, I would have died. I wouldn't mind if it were to take Mike-ko's place, but if I can't die without going through that agony, I wouldn't want to die for anyone's sake.

"But even for a cat, having a priest chant sutras and having a posthumous Buddhist name created, she should have no lingering regrets."

"Indeed, she is truly fortunate. But if I were to be picky, it seemed the priest's chanting was a bit too brief."

"It did seem a bit too short, so when I asked him about it, the priest from Gekkeiji-san said, 'Yes, I gave her a bit of the part that's effective; since she's a cat, that much is plenty for her to go to the Pure Land'."

"Oh my... but for that stray..."

I have often stated that I have no name, yet this maid keeps calling me "stray, stray." What an insolent creature.

"Since his sins are so deep, no matter how holy the sutras, his soul would never find rest."

I don't know how many hundreds of times "stray" was repeated after that. Abandoning this endless conversation halfway, I slipped off the cushion and jumped down from the veranda, my eighty-eight thousand, eight hundred and eighty hairs standing on end as I shivered. Since then, I have never approached the lute teacher's neighborhood. By now, the teacher herself is probably receiving a "brief" memorial service from Gekkeiji-san.

Lately, I lack the courage to go out. The world somehow feels tiresome. I have become as lazy a cat as my master. I have come to think it's no wonder people describe my master's shutting himself in his study as a broken heart.

Since I still haven't caught a rat, there was once a proposal from O-san to kick me out, but since the master knows I am no ordinary cat, I continue to live and idle away my days in this house. Regarding this point, I deeply appreciate the master's kindness and at the same time I do not hesitate to express my admiration for his discerning eye. I am not even angry that O-san mistreats me without knowing who I am. Someday, when a Hidari Jingoro appears and carves my portrait on the pillar of a temple gate, and a Japanese Steinlen takes a fancy to painting my likeness on canvas, those dull-witted fools will finally be ashamed of their own lack of discernment.
