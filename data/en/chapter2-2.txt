After the two had left, I took the liberty of helping myself to the remains of the kamaboko that Kangetsu-kun had bitten off. By now, I am no ordinary cat. I believe I am at least as qualified as the cat mentioned by Toen Momokawa, or perhaps the one that stole the gray goldfish. I certainly do not deign to consider Kuro of the rickshaw shop as an equal. No one would complain about me taking a single slice of fish cake. Besides, this habit of snacking in secret is not unique to us cats. Our maid often takes liberties with rice cakes and sweets whenever the mistress is out. It’s not just the maid, either; even the children, whom the mistress boasts are receiving a refined upbringing, show this tendency.

It happened four or five days ago. The two children woke up unusually early and sat across from each other at the dining table while the master and his wife were still asleep. Every morning, they usually eat a portion of the master's bread with sugar, but on this day, the sugar bowl had been left on the table with the spoon still inside. Since no one was there to distribute the sugar as usual, the older girl eventually scooped a spoonful from the bowl and poured it onto her plate. Then the younger one, following her sister's lead, put the same amount on her own plate in the same manner. For a while, they stared at each other, but then the older one took the spoon again and added another heap to her plate. The younger one immediately took the spoon and matched her sister's portion. Then the older sister scooped another. The younger sister, not to be outdone, added another. The sister reached for the bowl again; the younger one took the spoon again. As I watched, the heaps grew higher and higher, scoop by scoop, until finally, their plates were piled high with mountains of sugar and not a single grain remained in the bowl. Just then, the master emerged from the bedroom, rubbing his sleepy eyes, and promptly put the hard-won sugar back into the bowl. Seeing this, I thought that while humans may possess a sense of fairness derived from egoism that surpasses cats, in terms of wisdom, they are rather inferior. I thought they should have licked it up quickly before it piled so high, but as usual, my words would not reach them, so I simply watched in silence from atop the rice container, feeling a bit sorry for them.

Having gone out with Kangetsu-kun, the master returned late that night and didn't sit down at the breakfast table until about nine the next morning. Watching from my usual spot on the rice container, I saw the master eating ozoni in silence. He ate one bowl, then another. The pieces of mochi were small, but he must have eaten six or seven. He left the last piece in the bowl and put down his chopsticks, saying he had had enough. If anyone else were to be so selfish, the master would never allow it, but he, relishing his own authority, sat there unconcerned, looking at the charred corpse of the mochi in the muddy soup. When the mistress brought out the Taka-Diastase from the cabinet and placed it on the table, the master said, "That doesn't work, so I won't take it."

"But they say it is very effective for starchy foods, so you really should take it," the mistress insisted.

"Starch or no starch, it’s useless," he replied stubbornly.

"You really are a fickle person," the mistress muttered to herself.

"I’m not fickle; the medicine simply doesn't work."

"But the other day you were saying it worked wonderfully and took it every single day, didn't you?"

"It worked back then; it doesn't work now," he replied, as if reciting a formal verse.

"If you keep starting and stopping like that, no medicine, no matter how effective, will have a chance to work. You need a little more persistence, or else a stomach ailment, unlike other diseases, will never be cured," she said, turning to the maid who stood by with a tray.

"That is very true, sir. Unless you take it for a bit longer, you won't know if it's a good medicine or a bad one," the maid agreed immediately, siding with the mistress.

"Whatever, I’m not taking it because I’m not taking it! What do women know? Be quiet!"

"Well, I am just a woman," the mistress said, thrusting the Taka-Diastase in front of the master as if demanding he commit ritual suicide with it. The master said nothing, stood up, and went into his study. The mistress and the maid looked at each other and giggled. At a time like this, if I were to follow him and jump on his lap, I would surely suffer for it, so I circled around through the garden to the veranda of the study and peeked through a gap in the shoji. The master was reading a book by someone called Epictetus. If he could understand it as usual, he might have some merit. After five or six minutes, he slammed the book down on the desk. Thinking that was about what I expected, I continued to watch, and this time he took out his diary and wrote the following:

Took a walk with Kangetsu through Nezu, Ueno, Ikenohata, and Kanda. In front of a waiting house in Ikenohata, a geisha was playing battledore and shuttlecock in her New Year's finery. The clothes were beautiful, but her face was exceedingly ugly. Somehow, she resembled our cat.

There was really no need for him to use me as a specific example of an ugly face. If I went to the barber and had my face shaved, I wouldn't look all that different from a human. Humans are so conceited, and it’s quite troublesome.

Turning the corner at Hotan, another geisha appeared. She was slender with well-formed, sloping shoulders, and wore her light purple garments with an easy, elegant grace. Smiling with her white teeth showing, she said, 'Gen-chan, last night—I was just so busy...' However, her voice was as raspy as a traveling crow's, which made her fine appearance seem to plummet in value. It became too much trouble even to look back and see what sort of person this 'Gen-chan' was, so I walked on toward Onarichi with my hands in my sleeves. Kangetsu seemed somewhat restless.

Nothing is as difficult to understand as human psychology. I cannot tell if the master's heart right now is angry, or flighty, or if he is seeking a modicum of comfort in the legacy of a philosopher. I haven't the slightest idea whether he is sneering at the world, or wanting to join it, or getting worked up over trifles, or standing aloof from all things. Cats, in that regard, are simple. If we want to eat, we eat; if we want to sleep, we sleep. When we are angry, we are angry with all our might; when we cry, we cry as if it were the end of the world. First of all, we never keep such useless things as diaries. There is no need. For a person with two faces like the master, it may be necessary to write a diary to express in a dark room a side of himself that he cannot show to the world; but for us cats, our walking, staying, sitting, and lying, even our most basic bodily functions, are in themselves a true diary. Therefore, I see no need to go through such a tedious process to preserve my true character. If I have time to keep a diary, I’d rather just sleep on the veranda.

Had dinner at a certain restaurant in Kanda. After drinking two or three cups of Masamune for the first time in a while, my stomach feels excellent this morning. I believe an evening drink is the best thing for a weak stomach. Taka-Diastase is, of course, no good. No matter what anyone says, it’s useless. What doesn't work, simply doesn't work.

He attacks Taka-Diastase relentlessly. It’s as if he’s fighting a duel with himself. This morning's ill-temper shows its tail here. Perhaps this is where the true essence of a human diary lies.

The other day, OO said that skipping breakfast would improve the stomach, so I tried it for two or three days, but my stomach just growled and there was no effect. △△ advised me to definitely give up pickles. According to his theory, the source of all stomach ailments is pickled vegetables. He argued that if one gives up pickles, the source of the disease will be dried up, and recovery is certain. I didn't touch a pickle for about a week, but since no particular result was seen, I've started eating them again lately. According to XX, it must be abdominal massage. But not the ordinary kind. If one has it done once or twice in the Minagawa style, an old school of massage, most stomach ailments can be cured. Yasui Sokken was a great fan of this massage technique. Even heroes like Sakamoto Ryoma occasionally received treatment, they say, so I went off to Kami-Negishi at once to be massaged. However, the practitioner said that unless the bones are massaged, it won't heal, or that it’s hard to cure unless the positions of the internal organs are overturned, and he performed a truly brutal massage. Afterward, my body felt like cotton and I felt as if I had fallen into a sleeping sickness, so I gave it up after just one session. Mr. A insisted I should not eat solid food. So, I spent a day drinking nothing but milk, but my intestines made a gurgling sound like a great flood was occurring, and I couldn't sleep all night. Mr. B said that if I breathe with my diaphragm and exercise my internal organs, the stomach will naturally become healthy, so I should give it a try. I did this for a bit, but it made my insides feel uneasy and troubled. Besides, although I'd start it with total devotion whenever I remembered, I'd forget after five or six minutes. If I tried not to forget, I'd become so conscious of my diaphragm that I couldn't read a book or write a sentence. When the aesthetician Meitei saw me like this, he teased me, saying I wasn't a man in labor and should stop, so I’ve given it up lately. Mr. C said I should eat buckwheat noodles, so I immediately ate kake and mori buckwheat alternately, but this only gave me diarrhea and had no effect. I have tried every possible method to cure my long-standing stomach ailment, but all have failed. However, the three cups of Masamune I shared with Kangetsu last night certainly had an effect. From now on, I shall have two or three cups every night.

This, too, will surely not last long. The master's mind changes as incessantly as the pupils of my eyes. He is a man who can stick to nothing. Furthermore, despite worrying so much about his stomach in his diary, he puts on a great show of endurance in public, which is comical. The other day, a scholar friend of his came to visit and argued from a certain viewpoint that all illness is nothing but the result of the sins of one's ancestors and one's own sins. It was an impressive theory, well-researched, clear in logic, and orderly. Unfortunately, my master lacks the brainpower and scholarship to refute it. However, since he himself was suffering from a stomach ailment, he seemed to want to make some excuse to save face, so he gave a misplaced reply: "Your theory is interesting, but Carlyle had a weak stomach, you know," as if to say that because Carlyle was dyspeptic, his own dyspepsia was a badge of honor. To this, his friend snapped back, "Just because Carlyle had a weak stomach doesn't mean every dyspeptic patient can become a Carlyle," and the master fell silent. Although he is so full of vanity, in truth he obviously prefers not to have a weak stomach, so his starting an evening drink tonight is rather humorous. Come to think of it, his eating so much ozoni this morning might have been the influence of the Masamune he tossed back with Kangetsu-kun last night. I, too, suddenly felt like trying some ozoni.

I am a cat, but I eat most things. I don't have the energy to go on expeditions to the fishmonger's in the side alley like Kuro of the rickshaw shop, and I am certainly not in a position to be as picky as the calico cat at the home of the two-stringed lute teacher in Shimmichi. Consequently, I have surprisingly few dislikes. I eat the bread crumbs left by the children and lick the starch from rice cakes. Pickles are terribly unpalatable, but I once tried two slices of takuan for the experience. When I tried it, it was strange, but quite edible. To say "I don't like this" or "I don't like that" is a luxury of the selfish, and not something to be uttered by a cat in a teacher's house. According to the master, there was a novelist in France named Balzac. This man was a great seeker of luxury—though not in terms of food; being a novelist, he exhausted all luxuries of prose. One day, Balzac was trying to name a character in a novel he was writing, but nothing pleased him. Just then, a friend came to visit, and they went out for a walk together. The friend was taken out without knowing anything, of course, but Balzac was thinking of finding the name he was struggling with, so once they were on the street, he did nothing but look at the signs on the shops as they walked. However, he still couldn't find a name he liked. He walked his friend around relentlessly. The friend followed along, not knowing why. They eventually explored Paris from morning until night. On their way back, Balzac's eye was suddenly caught by the sign of a tailor. He saw the name "Marcus" written on the sign. Balzac clapped his hands and cried, "This is it! This is it! It must be this. Isn't Marcus a good name? I shall put the initial Z before Marcus, and then it will be a perfect name. It must be Z. Z. Marcus is truly excellent. No matter how well I think I've named a character myself, it always feels somewhat artificial and uninteresting. At last, I have a name I like!" He was delighted all by himself, completely forgetting the inconvenience to his friend. But to have to explore Paris for a whole day just to name a character in a novel is a very troublesome business. It would be fine if one could afford such luxuries, but in my position with an oyster-like master, I have no such inclinations. "Anything will do, as long as it’s edible"—this mindset must be the result of my circumstances. Therefore, my current desire for ozoni is by no means the result of luxury; rather, thinking that I should eat whatever I can while I can, I wondered if any of the ozoni the master had left over might still be in the kitchen.

I go around to the kitchen.

The same mochi I saw this morning is stuck to the bottom of the bowl in the same color I saw this morning. I must confess: I have never once had mochi in my mouth until now. Looking at it, it seems both delicious and a bit intimidating. I use my front paw to scrape aside the greens on top. When I look at my claws, the skin of the mochi is caught on them, feeling sticky. When I sniff it, it has an aroma like the rice from the bottom of the pot when it’s being moved to the container. Should I eat it? Should I not? I look around. Fortunately or unfortunately, no one is there. The maid is playing battledore and shuttlecock with the same face she has whether it’s the end of the year or the spring. In the inner room, the children are singing "What do you say, Mr. Rabbit?" If I am to eat it, now is the time. If I miss this chance, I shall have to live without knowing the taste of mochi until next year. At this moment, even as a cat, I realized a certain truth: "Rare opportunities compel all animals to venture upon things they do not even like."
