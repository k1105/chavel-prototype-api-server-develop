Kochi-kun downed his cooled tea and said with a formal air, "To tell the truth, the reason I’ve come today is to ask a small favor of you."

"I see. And what might that be?" the master replied, matching his formal tone.

"As you know, I am a lover of literature and the arts..."

"Excellent," the master added, fanning the flames.

"A group of like-minded people recently organized a 'Recitation Society.' We intend to meet once a month to continue our research in this field, and we already held our first meeting at the end of last year."

"May I ask—when you say 'Recitation Society,' it sounds like reading poetry or prose with a certain rhythm or melody. How exactly do you go about it?"

"Well, at first we started with the works of the ancients, but gradually we intend to include original works by our members."

"By 'works of the ancients,' do you mean something like Bai Juyi's The Song of the Pipa?"

"No."

"Is it something like Buson's The Song of the Spring Breeze on the Kema Embankment?"

"No."

"Then, what sort of thing did you do?"

"The other day, we did one of Chikamatsu’s love-suicide plays."

"Chikamatsu? You mean Chikamatsu the dramatist?" There is only one Chikamatsu. When one says Chikamatsu, it is bound to be the playwright. Thinking the master quite foolish for asking for clarification, I sat there as he politely stroked my head. In a world where some people believe they are loved by someone with a squint, such a misunderstanding is hardly surprising, so I let him stroke me as he pleased.

"Yes," Kochi-kun replied, observing the master’s expression.

"And do you recite it alone, or do you assign roles?"

"We assigned roles and did it as a dialogue. Our main objective is to sympathize with the characters and bring out their personalities as much as possible, accompanied by hand gestures and body movements. The primary goal of the speech is to portray the people of that era; whether it’s a young lady or a shop boy, we try to act as if that character has actually appeared."

"So, it’s rather like a play, isn't it?"

"Yes, it’s just that there are no costumes or stage scenery."

"Pardon me for asking, but does it go well?"

"Well, for a first meeting, I think it was a success."

"And what was the love-suicide play you said you did last time?"

"It was the part where a boatman takes a customer to Yoshiwara."

"You chose quite a dramatic scene," the master said, tilting his head slightly, as befits a teacher. The smoke from his 'Hinode' cigarette, blown from his nose, brushed past his ear and curled around the side of his face.

"Oh, it wasn't all that dramatic. The characters appearing were only the customer, the boatman, the courtesan, the waitress, the madam, and the manager," Kochi-kun said unconcernedly. The master made a slightly sour face at the mention of a courtesan, but since he lacked a clear understanding of terms like 'waitress,' 'madam,' and 'manager,' he first posed a question.

"Is a 'waitress' equivalent to a maidservant in a brothel?"

"I haven't researched it thoroughly yet, but a 'waitress' is a maid at a teahouse, and I believe a 'madam' is something like an assistant in the women's quarters." Although Kochi-kun had just said they use vocal characterization to make the characters appear, it seemed he didn't quite understand the personalities of a madam or a waitress.

"I see. So a waitress belongs to a teahouse, while a madam lives and works in a brothel. Next, is the 'manager' a person, or does it refer to a specific place? If it’s a person, is it a man or a woman?"

"I believe the manager is a man."

"And what is he in charge of?"

"Well, my research hasn't reached that far yet. I'll look into it soon."

If they performed a dialogue like this, I thought it would surely turn out to be a total mess, and I looked up at the master's face. The master was unexpectedly serious.

"And who else joined you as reciters besides yourself?"

"There were various people. The courtesan was played by Mr. K, a Bachelor of Laws, but since he has a mustache and was using a sweet, feminine voice, it was a bit strange. Moreover, there’s a part where the courtesan has a fit of spasms..."

"Do you have to have a fit of spasms even in a recitation?" the master asked worriedly.

"Yes, because expression is the most important thing," Kochi-kun said, maintaining his air of a man of letters.

"And did the spasms come off well?" the master quipped.

"A fit of spasms was a bit much for the first meeting," Kochi-kun quipped back.

"By the way, what was your role?" the master asked.

"I was the boatman."

"Oh, you were the boatman?" The master's tone seemed to imply that if Kochi-kun could manage being a boatman, then he himself could surely handle being a manager. Soon he added, with his usual lack of flattery, "Wasn't the boatman a bit beyond you?"

Kochi-kun didn't seem particularly offended. In his calm tone, he said, "It was because of that boatman that the much-anticipated event ended in an anticlimax. Actually, four or five schoolgirls were lodging next door to the venue. Somehow they found out there was a recitation society that day and came to listen under the window. I was using a boatman’s voice and getting into the rhythm, thinking I was doing quite well, when... well, perhaps my gestures were too much, for the schoolgirls, who had been holding it in until then, all burst out laughing at once. I was surprised and embarrassed, and my flow was broken; I simply couldn't continue after that, so we finally adjourned."

If this was a "success" for a first meeting, I wondered what a failure would be like, and I couldn't help but laugh. Unwittingly, my Adam's apple began to purr. The master stroked my head even more gently. It is gratifying to be treated with affection for laughing at people, but it is also a bit unsettling.

"That was quite a pity," the master said, offering his condolences so early in the New Year.

"From the second meeting onwards, we intend to exert ourselves even more and make it a grand affair. The reason I came today was entirely for that; I would like to ask you, Sensei, to join us and lend us your support."

"I certainly can't have a fit of spasms," the master, ever passive, immediately began to refuse.

"No, you don't need to have spasms or anything of the sort. I have here a list of supporting members..." As he spoke, Kochi-kun carefully took a small notebook from a purple wrapping cloth. "I would be honored if you would sign and seal this," he said, leaving the notebook open on the master’s lap. Looking at it, I saw a neat lineup of names of currently famous Doctors of Literature and Bachelors of Literature.

"Hmph. I suppose I could become a supporting member, but what are the obligations?" the "Oyster Teacher" asked, looking concerned.

"In the way of obligations, there is nothing in particular we would ask of you. It would be enough if you would just enter your name and signify your approval."

"In that case, I’ll join," the master said, becoming suddenly lighthearted as soon as he learned there were no duties. His expression seemed to say that if he were sure there was no responsibility, he would even sign his name to a rebel manifesto. Furthermore, to have his name listed among such famous scholars was a supreme honor for a master who had never encountered such an opportunity before, so it was no wonder his reply was so energetic.

"Excuse me a moment," the master said, going into his study to get his seal. I tumbled down onto the tatami. Kochi-kun picked up a piece of sponge cake from the dish and stuffed it into his mouth in one go. For a while, he seemed to be struggling as he chewed. I was reminded of my own ozoni incident this morning. By the time the master emerged from his study with his seal, the sponge cake had settled into Kochi-kun’s stomach. The master didn't seem to notice that one piece of cake was missing from the dish. If he did notice, I would surely be the first one suspected.

After Kochi-kun left, the master went into his study, and looking at his desk, he found that a letter from Meitei-san had arrived in the meantime.

"I offer my congratulations for the New Year. ..."

The master thought the opening was unusually serious. There are almost no serious letters from Meitei-san; recently, one had arrived saying, "Since then, I have found no woman to fall in love with, and no love letters have arrived from any quarter, so I am just managing to pass my time in peace. Please rest your mind on my behalf." Compared to that, this New Year’s greeting was exceptionally conventional.

"I wished to pay a visit, but contrary to your passive philosophy, I have planned to welcome this unprecedented New Year with as active a policy as possible, and as a result, I am dizzyingly busy every single day, as you might imagine. ..."

Indeed, being that man, he must be busy playing around during the New Year, the master agreed in his mind.

"Yesterday, I stole a moment of leisure and intended to treat Kochi-kun to a feast of 'Tochimenbo,' but unfortunately, due to a shortage of ingredients, I was unable to fulfill my intention, much to my regret. ..."

He’s starting up again, the master thought, smiling silently.

"Tomorrow is a card party at a certain Baron's; the day after, the New Year banquet of the Aesthetic Society; the day after that, a welcome party for Professor Toribe; and the day after that... "

How annoying, the master thought, skipping ahead.

"As mentioned above, due to a non-stop series of Noh meetings, Haiku meetings, Tanka meetings, and New-Style Poetry meetings, I shall be attending them without a break for the time being. I beg your kind forgiveness for substituting this letter for a personal visit. ..."

There’s no need for you to come anyway, the master replied to the letter.

"The next time you favor me with a visit, I intend to provide a dinner for the first time in a while. My humble kitchen has no delicacies, but I am already setting my heart on providing at least some 'Tochimenbo.' ..."

He’s still harping on about Tochimenbo. How insolent, the master thought, feeling a bit huffy.

"However, since ingredients for Tochimenbo have been scarce lately, it is possible it might not be ready in time. In that case, I shall serve you some peacock tongues. ..."

He’s hedging his bets, the master thought, wanting to read on.

"As you know, for each peacock, the amount of tongue meat is less than half a little finger. To fill the stomach of a hearty eater like yourself... "

What a liar, the master said, as if tossing the letter aside.

"I believe I must capture at least twenty or thirty peacocks. However, while peacocks can be seen here and there at the zoo or the Asakusa Hanayashiki, they are nowhere to be found at ordinary poulterers, which is quite a struggle. ..."

You’re just struggling for your own amusement, the master thought, showing no gratitude whatsoever.

"This dish of peacock tongues was extremely popular for a time during the height of the Roman Empire. Please understand that as a peak of luxury and refinement, it has long secretly whetted my appetite. ..."

Understand what? How stupid, the master thought, quite coldly.

"Later, until the sixteenth or seventeenth century, peacocks were an indispensable delicacy at banquets throughout Europe. I recall that peacocks were used when the Earl of Leicester invited Queen Elizabeth to Kenilworth. In a famous banquet scene painted by Rembrandt, a peacock lies on the table with its tail fully spread... "

If you have time to write a history of peacock cuisine, you can't be all that busy, the master grumbled.

"In any case, with this continuous feasting lately, even I am bound to become dyspeptic like you before long... "

"Like you" is unnecessary. There’s no need to make me the standard for dyspepsia, the master muttered.

"According to historians, the Romans held banquets two or three times a day. If even a man with a strong stomach sits down to a massive feast two or three times a day, his digestive functions are bound to be thrown out of order, and consequently, naturally, he will become like you... "

Again, "like you"? How rude.

"However, having researched how to balance luxury and health, they recognized the need to maintain their stomachs and intestines in a normal state while simultaneously craving an inordinate amount of delicacies, and they devised a secret method... "

Oh? the master suddenly became interested.

"They always took a bath after a meal. After the bath, by a certain method, they would vomit up everything they had swallowed before the bath, thereby clearing out their stomachs. After achieving this gastric purification, they would sit down at the table again and enjoy delicacies to their heart's content. When finished, they would go into the bath again and vomit. In this way, they could indulge in their favorite foods as much as they liked without causing any harm to their internal organs. I believe this might be what is meant by 'killing two birds with one stone.' ..."

Indeed, it is certainly killing two birds with one stone, the master thought, looking envious.

"In the twentieth century, with the frequency of travel and the increase in banquets—not to mention the many affairs of a military nation in this second year of the war with Russia—I am convinced that we, the citizens of a victorious nation, have reached the moment where we must study this Roman art of bathing and vomiting. Otherwise, I secretly fear that all the citizens of this great nation will, in the near future, become stomach patients like you... "

Again, "like you"! What an irritating man, the master thought.

"At this juncture, those of us familiar with Western affairs should research ancient history and legends, discover this lost secret method, and apply it to Meiji society. This would serve as a merit to prevent disaster before it arises, and also act as a repayment for the pleasures we normally enjoy. ..."

Something is fishy, the master thought, tilting his head.

"Therefore, I have been leafing through the works of Gibbon, Mommsen, Smith, and others lately, but it is a great regret that I have yet to find even a clue to its discovery. However, as you know, once I set my mind on something, I never stop until I succeed. I believe the revival of the art of vomiting is not far off. I shall inform you as soon as I discover it, so please be advised. Regarding the treat of Tochimenbo and peacock tongues I mentioned earlier, I would like to serve them after this discovery if possible; that would be convenient for me, of course, and also, I believe, beneficial for you, who are already suffering from dyspepsia. Yours in haste."

"What? So he was pulling my leg after all? He wrote it so seriously that I ended up reading it in earnest until the very end. Meitei must be a man of great leisure to play such a prank so early in the New Year," the master said with a laugh.
